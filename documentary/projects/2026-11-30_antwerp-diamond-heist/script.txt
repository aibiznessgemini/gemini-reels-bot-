In 2003, thieves broke into one of the most secure vaults on Earth.
A half-eaten sandwich gave them away.
Antwerp, Belgium. A city where most of the world's rough diamonds pass through.
In its diamond district, beneath the Antwerp Diamond Centre, lay the vault.
It was protected by layer after layer of security: guards, cameras, heat and motion sensors, and a massive steel door.
For years, a man named Leonardo Notarbartolo had worked in the building.
He rented an office there, and posed as an Italian gem trader.
That gave him something no outsider had: time, and a key card.
On the weekend of February 15, 2003, a small crew went down to the vault.
They got past the defences, one by one.
By Monday morning, more than a hundred safe deposit boxes had been emptied.
Diamonds, gold, jewellery and cash were gone. Estimates of the haul run as high as a hundred million dollars.
It is often called the largest diamond heist in history.
But the getaway was not as careful as the break-in.
Near a motorway outside Antwerp, the thieves dumped a bag of rubbish in a patch of woods.
A local man found it, and called the police.
Inside were vault documents, video tape from the building, and a half-eaten salami sandwich.
The sandwich carried DNA.
Notarbartolo was arrested, and sentenced to ten years in prison.
Several other members of the crew were convicted too.
But most of the diamonds were never found.
To this day, no one has explained exactly how the vault was opened.
One of the most secure rooms on Earth was beaten by planning, patience and inside access.
And the whole plan was undone by lunch.
