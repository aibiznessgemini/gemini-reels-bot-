In the summer of 1518, a woman stepped into a street in Strasbourg and began to dance.
She could not stop.
Her name was recorded as Frau Troffea.
There was no music. No celebration. She simply danced, for hours, until she collapsed.
The next day, she got up and danced again.
Within days, others joined her. Dozens of them.
By the end of the summer, some accounts say hundreds of people were dancing in the streets.
They danced until their feet bled. Some reportedly danced until they died.
The city council called in doctors.
The doctors said it was a natural illness, caused by hot blood.
And the cure, they said, was more dancing.
So the city cleared halls, built a wooden stage, and hired musicians.
They hoped the dancers would dance the sickness out.
It made things worse. Watching the dancers seemed to draw in even more.
The authorities changed their minds.
They banned public music and dancing.
Finally, the dancers were taken to a shrine of Saint Vitus, in the hills outside the city.
And slowly, by the end of summer, the plague came to an end.
To this day, no one knows for certain what happened.
Some suggested poisoning from mouldy grain.
But many historians point to something stranger: the power of fear.
Strasbourg had suffered years of famine, disease and despair.
People believed an angry saint could curse them with dancing.
When belief and fear are strong enough, a body can follow the mind.
Five hundred years later, we still have a name for it: mass psychogenic illness.
The town that could not stop dancing was not mad.
It was terrified.
