In 1971, a man in a dark suit hijacked a plane, collected two hundred thousand dollars, and jumped into the night.
He was never seen again.
November 24, 1971. The day before Thanksgiving.
At Portland airport, a quiet man bought a one-way ticket to Seattle, under the name Dan Cooper.
He sat at the back of the plane, ordered a drink, and handed a flight attendant a note.
She thought he was flirting. Then he leaned in and told her to read it.
He had a bomb in his briefcase.
He showed her wires and red sticks inside it.
He wanted two hundred thousand dollars in twenty-dollar bills, and four parachutes.
In Seattle, the FBI handed over the cash. They had recorded the serial numbers.
Cooper let the passengers go.
Then he ordered the crew to fly south, slowly, and low.
Somewhere over the forests of south-west Washington, he lowered the rear stairs of the plane.
And he jumped. At night, in freezing rain, with the money strapped to his body.
The plane landed in Reno. The back of the cabin was empty.
The FBI searched the woods for weeks. No body. No parachute. No money.
The press called him D. B. Cooper, after an early mix-up, and the name stuck.
Then, in 1980, an eight-year-old boy was playing on a sandy riverbank called Tena Bar.
He dug up bundles of rotting twenty-dollar bills.
Five thousand eight hundred dollars. The serial numbers matched.
It was the first real clue in nine years, and it only made the mystery deeper.
How did the money get there? Where was the rest?
Hundreds of suspects were investigated over the decades.
In 2016, the FBI stopped actively working the case.
It remains the only unsolved hijacking in American history.
Somewhere, the rest of the money may still be out there.
