She was never sick a day in her life.
But wherever she cooked, people fell ill.
In the summer of 1906, typhoid fever struck a wealthy family renting a house on Long Island.
Typhoid was a disease of dirty water and crowded slums, not of summer mansions.
So the owners hired an investigator, a sanitation engineer named George Soper.
Soper checked the water, the milk, the drains. All clean.
Then he looked at the staff. The family had hired a new cook, just before the outbreak.
Her name was Mary Mallon.
She had left soon after the family fell ill. She did that often.
Soper traced her work history, house by house, family by family.
In home after home, typhoid had followed her.
His theory was shocking for its time: a healthy person could carry the disease, and pass it on.
In 1907, he found her working in a house in Manhattan, and asked for samples.
Mary was furious. She had never been ill. She chased him away.
Health officials came back with police. She fought, and hid, and was dragged into an ambulance.
Tests found typhoid bacteria in her body.
She was isolated on North Brother Island, in New York's East River.
In 1910, she was released, on one condition: she must never work as a cook again.
But cooking was the only good job she had.
In 1915, typhoid broke out at a maternity hospital in Manhattan.
The new cook had been working under the name Mrs. Brown.
It was Mary Mallon.
She was sent back to the island, and stayed there for the rest of her life.
She died in 1938.
The newspapers called her Typhoid Mary, and the name became a byword for danger.
But she was also the first person in America identified as a healthy carrier.
Her story still raises a hard question: how much freedom should one person lose, to protect everyone else?
