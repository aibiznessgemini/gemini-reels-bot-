In 1925, a man sold the Eiffel Tower. He did not own it.
His name was Victor Lustig, and he may have been the smoothest con man who ever lived.
That spring, he read a small newspaper story about the tower.
It said the tower needed costly repairs, and that Paris was struggling to keep it up.
The tower had been built for the World's Fair of 1889. It was never meant to stand forever.
Lustig saw an opportunity.
He had official-looking stationery printed, and became a deputy director of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs.
Then he took a suite at the Hôtel de Crillon, one of the grandest hotels in Paris.
He invited the city's biggest scrap metal dealers to a secret meeting.
His message was simple. The government was selling the Eiffel Tower, for scrap.
It had to stay secret, he said, or the public would be outraged.
He even drove the dealers to the tower in a rented limousine.
Then he asked for bids.
But Lustig was not looking for the highest price. He was looking for the easiest victim.
He found him: André Poisson, a dealer desperate to be taken seriously in Paris.
Poisson's wife was suspicious. So Lustig made a confession.
He was only a poorly paid official, he said, and a man in his position needed a little help.
Poisson understood. A bribe. Only a real official would dare to ask for one.
So Poisson paid the bribe, and then he paid for the tower.
Lustig took the money and left for Austria.
Then he waited, reading the Paris newspapers every day.
Nothing. Poisson was too embarrassed to go to the police.
Lustig went on to swindle people across Europe and America.
In 1935 he was arrested in the United States for counterfeiting money.
He was sent to Alcatraz, and he died in federal custody in 1947.
The Eiffel Tower is still standing.
The man who sold it is a footnote.
But his trick still works today: make the lie official, make it secret, and make the victim feel chosen.
