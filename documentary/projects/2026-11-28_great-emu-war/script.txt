In 1932, Australia sent soldiers and machine guns to fight an enemy.
The enemy won. It was a bird.
After the First World War, thousands of veterans were given land in Western Australia to grow wheat.
Then the Great Depression hit, and wheat prices collapsed.
And then came the emus.
Every year, emus migrate toward the coast after breeding season.
In 1932, huge numbers of them found the farmland, with its fresh water and ripe crops.
They ate the wheat, trampled the fields, and broke through the fences.
The farmers, many of them ex-soldiers, knew exactly what they wanted. Machine guns.
The government agreed.
Major Meredith of the Royal Australian Artillery was sent with two soldiers, two Lewis guns, and ten thousand rounds of ammunition.
It did not go to plan.
First, it rained, and the birds scattered across a wider area.
When the soldiers finally opened fire, the emus split into small groups and ran.
They could run fast, and they did not stay in range.
In one ambush, a gun jammed after only a few birds were hit.
The newspapers loved it. Parliament joked about it.
Within days, the soldiers were called back.
Then the farmers complained again, and the army returned for a second try.
This time, Major Meredith reported far more success.
He claimed nine hundred and eighty-six birds, using nine thousand eight hundred and sixty rounds.
That was ten bullets for every emu.
And still, the emus kept coming.
Later, farmers turned to bounties and better fences instead of machine guns.
The army had modern weapons, a plan, and a commander.
The emus had numbers, speed, and an endless supply of wheat.
History remembers who won.
