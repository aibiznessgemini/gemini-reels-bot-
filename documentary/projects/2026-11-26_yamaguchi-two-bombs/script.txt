On August 6, 1945, Tsutomu Yamaguchi survived an atomic bomb.
Three days later, he survived another one.
He was a twenty-nine-year-old engineer who designed ships for Mitsubishi.
That summer, his company sent him from his home in Nagasaki to Hiroshima, on business.
August 6 was supposed to be his last day there.
At a quarter past eight in the morning, he was walking to the shipyard.
He heard a plane. He looked up, and saw a tiny object falling.
Then the sky turned white.
He was less than two miles from the blast.
The light burned his face and arms. The shock wave threw him to the ground.
His eardrums were ruptured, and he could barely see.
He spent the night in an air-raid shelter, in a city that no longer existed.
The next day, wrapped in bandages, he boarded a train home, to Nagasaki.
On the morning of August 9, he went back to work.
His boss asked him what had happened in Hiroshima.
How could one bomb destroy a whole city?
As Yamaguchi described the flash, the room filled with the same white light.
Once again, he was about two miles from the centre of the blast.
Once again, he survived. So did his wife and his baby son.
For years, he hardly spoke about what he had lived through.
Many survivors were recorded for one bombing. He was recorded for Nagasaki only.
In 2009, Japan officially recognised him as a survivor of both.
He is the only person the government has ever recognised this way.
In his last years, he travelled to speak against nuclear weapons.
He died in 2010, at the age of ninety-three.
Two cities. Three days. One man who lived to tell the world never to let it happen again.
