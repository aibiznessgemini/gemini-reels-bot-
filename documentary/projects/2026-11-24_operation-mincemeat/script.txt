In 1943, one of the most important British officers in the war had already been dead for months.
And he had never been an officer at all.
The Allies were about to invade Sicily, and everyone knew it.
So two British intelligence officers, Ewen Montagu and Charles Cholmondeley, planned a lie.
They would give Germany a dead man carrying secret plans.
The body belonged to Glyndwr Michael, a homeless Welshman who had died after swallowing rat poison.
They turned him into someone who never existed: Major William Martin, of the Royal Marines.
They gave him an identity card, a uniform, and a whole private life.
Love letters from a fiancée. Her photograph. Receipts. Ticket stubs.
Then they chained a briefcase to his belt.
Inside was a letter between two generals, hinting that the real targets were Greece and Sardinia.
The body was packed in dry ice and loaded onto a submarine.
On April 30, 1943, off the coast of Huelva in Spain, the crew slipped it into the sea.
A Spanish fisherman found him floating near the shore.
Spain was neutral, but full of German agents.
The papers in the briefcase were opened, copied, and passed on to German intelligence.
Then the briefcase was quietly returned to the British, as if nothing had happened.
In London, the planners waited to see if the lie had worked.
In July 1943, the Allies landed on Sicily.
The island fell faster than expected, and with fewer losses than planned.
No one can measure exactly how much one dead man changed.
But the deception became one of the most famous of the war.
Glyndwr Michael was buried in Huelva, under another man's name.
In 1998, his real name was finally added to the stone.
He was a man nobody noticed in life.
In death, he walked straight into enemy headquarters.
