This empty wall turned a quiet portrait into the most famous painting in the world.
It happened on Monday, August 21, 1911.
That day, the Louvre was closed to the public.
But a man in a white worker's smock was already inside.
He walked into the Salon Carré and lifted a small portrait off its hooks.
It was painted on a wooden panel, just 77 by 53 centimetres.
On a service staircase, he stripped away its glass case and frame.
Then he hid the panel under his smock, and walked out.
Nobody noticed. Not that day.
The next morning, a painter named Louis Béroud came to sketch her.
Where the Mona Lisa should have been, he found only four iron pegs.
The Louvre shut its doors for a whole week.
And newspapers printed her face again, and again, and again.
Police even arrested the poet Guillaume Apollinaire.
They questioned his friend, a young painter called Pablo Picasso.
Both men were cleared.
When the museum reopened, crowds came just to stare at the empty wall.
Among them was a young writer from Prague: Franz Kafka.
Meanwhile, detectives knocked on the door of the real thief.
His name was Vincenzo Peruggia, an Italian who had once worked at the Louvre.
He had even helped build the glass case that was meant to protect her.
The police believed his alibi, and they left.
For two years, the Mona Lisa lay hidden in a trunk in his room.
Then, in late 1913, he wrote to an art dealer in Florence.
The dealer, Alfredo Geri, brought the director of the Uffizi Gallery to see it.
They checked the panel, confirmed it was real, and called the police.
In December 1913, Peruggia was arrested.
He said he only wanted to bring her home to Italy.
The court was lenient. He spent only months in prison.
In January 1914, the Mona Lisa returned to the Louvre.
Before the theft, she was not even the most visited painting in the museum.
Today, she is the most famous painting on Earth.
All because, for two years, she was missing.
