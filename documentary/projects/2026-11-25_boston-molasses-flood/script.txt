On a winter day in 1919, a wave swept through the streets of Boston. It was not water.
It was molasses. More than two million gallons of it.
January 15, 1919. Lunchtime in the North End, one of the busiest neighbourhoods in the city.
Above the harbour stood a giant steel tank, full of molasses waiting to be turned into alcohol.
Neighbours said it leaked. Its brown streaks ran down the sides.
Shortly after noon, people heard a deep rumble, and a sound like machine guns.
The rivets were bursting.
The tank split open, and a brown wall of syrup rolled into the street.
Scientists later estimated the first wave moved at up to 35 miles per hour.
It smashed buildings, lifted a train off its tracks, and swallowed horses, carts and people.
And molasses does not let go.
In the cold air it grew thick, trapping anyone who fell into it.
Rescuers waded in up to their waists. Some had to cut people free.
Twenty-one people died. About a hundred and fifty were injured.
The cleanup took weeks. The harbour ran brown.
The company blamed anarchists. It said someone had blown up the tank.
But in court, the evidence told another story.
The tank had been built too fast, with steel too thin, and was never properly tested.
The company was held responsible and had to pay the victims.
After the disaster, states across the country adopted stricter building rules.
Engineers had to sign their work, and prove that their structures were safe.
Today, a small plaque marks the spot.
Some Bostonians still say that on hot summer days, the old streets smell faintly sweet.
