They were told it was safe to put the glowing paint in their mouths.
It was not.
During the First World War, soldiers needed watches they could read in the dark.
The answer was a new miracle paint, made with radium.
In a factory in Orange, New Jersey, young women painted tiny numbers onto watch dials.
The brushes were thin, and the numbers were small.
To keep a sharp point, the women shaped each brush with their lips. Dip, point, paint.
Some of them asked if it was dangerous.
Their supervisors told them it was safe.
Radium was sold as a health tonic. People drank it. Some put it on their skin.
The women even painted their nails and teeth with it, for fun. They glowed.
Then, one by one, they started to fall ill.
Their teeth fell out. Their jaws began to crumble. Their bones broke.
Doctors were baffled. The company blamed other causes.
Radium had settled in their bones, like calcium, and was destroying them from the inside.
In 1927, one of the painters, Grace Fryer, found a young lawyer willing to take the case.
Four more women joined her. The newspapers called them the Radium Girls.
They were running out of time, and everyone knew it.
In 1928, the company settled out of court.
Each woman received ten thousand dollars, a yearly pension, and her medical bills.
Grace Fryer died in 1933.
But the case changed the rules.
Workers gained the right to sue for damage caused by their jobs.
And the science of radiation safety grew out of their suffering.
The watches stopped glowing long ago.
The women who painted them still light the way.
